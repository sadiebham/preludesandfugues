\prelude

Piper\=was. She was\=what? She fluttered her wings and cheeped inquisitively. She was.

She fluttered her wings again, rising off the ground a little way. She was in a forest and sunlight was slanting through the trees. The air smelt of earth and acorns and optimism\=she trilled excitedly, turning too fast and nearly falling, confusing her wings with her hands for a moment before regaining her balance.

There was a path running through the trees. The trees were not thickly spaced but they did grow tall. She tilted her head back and back until she was tumbling downwards again and squawked in distress, in surprise, gaining the ground without harm. She looked up again. Sat down. Lay down, so she could properly see the sky.

It was blue and gorgeous, with brown-limbed trees and branches caressing it, with leaves in all shades of hues intermingling with it, until she wasn’t sure where tree ended and sky began.

She chirped in delight.

Cheeped.

Squawked.

`I wonder\='

She chirruped softly. There were sounds, and they were her.

`I can speak!' she chirped, turning her head this way and that to see if anyone had heard her, if there was anyone else nearby.

There wasn't, but that didn't mean\=

`I can talk!' she cheeped delightedly.

\fugue

Part golden, sepia light spilled through the forest.

What she had taken first\=upon becoming\=as trees, Piper realised now were so much more. They grew out of the ground, in the shape of trees, but the lower trunk and branches were bookcases and shelves and contained within them were tales, written on leaves and bark, scrolls and parchment and books themselves, printed from pulped paper, but everywhere, books were part of the trees.

The air smelled of acorns and parchment and as she fluttered closer to an old oak, she saw he had bookcases all around his trunk. The books within were brightly coloured in the colours of autumn, reds and yellows, browns and dusky blues, the text in black, like veins or the capillaries of a leaf, edged in gold.

Piper lifted one experimentally. The tree stirred, shedding a pair of leaves that slowly spiralled downwards, as if acknowledging her opening the book.

The skin of the book was bark, rough against her hands. She fluttered her wings excitedly, closing her eyes and tilting her head back, splaying her fingers across a page. The page felt like a leaf, brittle and ephemeral, fragile, but containing so much.

She whistled wordlessly.

The next book was a scroll, an unwrapping of bark, but a bark that crackled and creaked like an old oak, caught in a storm. The letters were etched in black, but lined with gold: the colour of autumnal nights.

Piper whistled. The lettering was lovely.

She ran a fingertip along the curls and slants. The shapes defined: she didn't know what they all meant, but there was beauty in them and beauty in the not-knowing.

She wrapped it up again carefully and returned it to its place on the shelf.

She fluttered back down to the ground. There were leaves scattered all around: golden and red and brown, pale green and dark yellow.

She gave a half-cheep of delight and flung herself down, extending her legs and her wings this way and that before jumping back into the air.

`I'm an autumn-leaf-angel' she chirped, flapping her wings excitedly, watching the leaves scatter and wash over each other, watching them stir and move as if dancing, as if pulled by invisible strings.

A gentle breeze blew.

She flew into it, feeling the wind pass between her feathers, feeling the wind move around her, feeling it lean into her, forcing her to flap harder.

She spiralled down around a weeping willow, the air cooler, stiller, the wind less noticeable. The grass was greener, deeper, softer, with patches of moss and tendrils of willow wisping gently in the breeze or by its own accord.

She touched down gently, shaking her wings. The ground was softer and the air was damper, smelling of earth and mud and water. She walked around, looking this way and that, looking for the stream, listening intently for the sound of water tinkling across rocks, but there was no sound of water, only the presence of water. %SPK

There were few leaves around the willow. No second go at being a leaf-angel, and she thought the dampness of the grass and the moss might get into her wings. Piper fluttered her wings.

There were no books in this part of the forest either. The willow tree was a willow tree.

She fluttered up again. The wind had eased down, and though, carrying on in the direction she had started, the trees grew together thicker, there was also a more intense golden light spilling through the high branches and the leaves, cascading down in slanting beams of yellow and orange and red. Here, leaves spiralled down blissfully, coasting between the trees, as if a gentle breeze carried them safely to the ground.

She fluttered towards the light. There was a misty, ghostly, soft feel to it, as if the sunlight were being streamed through fog.

As the trees neared, she saw that instead of shelves with books, as previous trees had been, these trees had smaller doorways set within them, each containing a single book. She fluttered up to one, staring in surprise. %SPK

\textit{The Adventure of Time}, she read, looking around wildly.

She was alone in the book-forest, as far as she could see.

No one else was around to have spoken.

`I\=' she stared at the book-within-the-tree. `I can read words?'

She could read the author's name. Opened the cover with her wingtip, and words jumped out at her in a tangle of consonants and vowels. The letters were tighter and smaller than the previous book she had looked at, and\=there was something a little depressing about them, Piper felt.

In the other book, with its black and gold lettering, the letters were free and rounded and swirled this way and that in beautiful curves. The words in this book all stood in a line, shoulder-to-shoulder, as if rounded up and corralled to not stand out from one another: to present a united front.

She squawked disconsolately.

`I can read letters,' she cheeped softly. `But they look trapped and lined up.'

She moved another page on, turning the crisp, cream, parchment with her wingtip. The words were more broken up, following. They still had a uniform, hard edge, but there were lines that weren't full of words and could breathe, and there were gaps between paragraphs and stars, as if the author wanted to draw the reader's attention away from the text to another place.

Piper cheeped, a little happier.

She closed the book and flapped over to another tree. This tree was broader, thicker, and the doorways were larger, the books within larger, and there were even little seats in front of the books.

She opened the book carefully. For such a large, impressive tree, with grey-brown bark faintly glowing in a soft-golden light, the book cover was pale blue, the blue of a sunrise, not quite evolved, but caught and reflected across water, splashed through with curls of golden light.

She trilled with delight. The words were larger, given free rein within the page, and there were bright images accompanying the text. The words' meaning was just there: a tale for a child, for one young and curious, of delightful things to explore and find.

She turned the page.

The tale continued and now she could read the words without needing to run the edge of her wingtip across the curls and curlicues. The story was\=she tweeted excitedly. She didn't have to think about it! The tale was forming in her mind as her eyes beheld the lettering.
She cheeped excitedly, closing her eyes and leaning back a little.

Wisps of sunlight warmed her wings.

Piper launched backwards off the tree, spinning in the air for a moment, the sunlight catching and warming her wings. She carried on spinning backwards, enjoying the feel of being slightly out of control, of being warmed by the sun, of being surrounded by\=words and tales and stories that she could read!

She cheeped, regaining her balance. There were tall trees all around her, each offering a plethora of books to read, some large and beautifully colourful, others densely and tightly packed with uniform letters.

`I can read anything I want!'

Piper fluttered forwards. There are enough books here, she thought, I can spend the rest of my life reading and\=a little cross-breeze distracted her and she quarter-turned, surprised, and saw that to the left of her, the book-forest extended, but the golden light bled down to a foggy-white light, though the space between the trees was still bright and intriguing.

She allowed the breeze to carry her towards the foggy light.

The trees were very similar, though smaller. The air was cooler, crisper, sharper almost. It wasn't cold, as she'd expected, the light seemed clearer though less warm in colour.
`How big is this book-forest?'

She fluttered into the crisper, foggy-white light. The trees had more, smaller, branches, and at the end of each small branch was a door, smaller than the ones in the golden-lighted forest, but still each held a book, the doors all open so that she could peruse.

She fluttered across to the nearest one. The light was softer, gentler. The book was about a journey, she realised, though it was a large book and the text was crammed in very tight. She could smell excitement and pain from the book; swathes of scenic description and human endurance.

She wriggled her beak a little. It was a very big book. It would take me a very long time to read that.

She fluttered over to another branch and another door.

The book tasted of courage and confidence. There were pastel images and a mixture of solid text, though in much smaller groupings, and curved text to go with the images.

She opened the book further, scenting a tale of friendship, of overcoming adversity.

Can others do this, she wondered. Do they have to read all the text or can they tell what the story is about from a page or two?

She closed her eyes. The book was lovely and she could feel the warmth from it, a little brighter than the foggy sunlight that cloaked the trees.

The warmth seeped into her.

Should I go back to the start and read the books in order?

Should I continue randomly flying from tree to tree and seeing what books the trees have to offer me?

Her breathing slowed. The tree was breathing, she could feel. Soaking in sunlight. Drawing in moisture from the earth it was buried deep in. Patterns of communication ran between the trees, unseen, unheard. This could be my home, she thought. I could stay here and there would always be something new to read, and warm places to sleep, and sunlight and rain, occasionally, if the temperature became too hot\=or maybe I would move next to the willow for a while, where it was warmer and damper, where the ground was softer.

I could stay here, and never see what else there is to discover.

She frowned, in her almost-asleep mode. I want to see what's out there.

I like the forest, but there is so much more. And I can read now.

She shifted her footing, waking herself up.

The ground was warm. The branch was warm. The light was soft and muted. She could fall asleep anywhere and knew she wouldn't fall to the ground: and even if she did, the ground and the trees and the books would catch her and she would be safe.

Piper squawked, looking around.

In the distance, she could see the edge of the golden-lighted trees. That way was beautiful, both the trees and the air and also the books. But I've been that way.

She looked around. Further away, but pushing on in the same general sort of direction, the foggy-white light seemed to deepen to a strangely blue-tinged light.

She launched herself off the branch, chirping inquisitively. The foggy light swirled around her, windless, but didn't hinder her as she set off for the strangely hued blue light.

It came nearer and nearer and with it came the scent of salt spray, and strange, splintering, crashing sounds.

Are trees falling she thought, in trepidation and horror. I don't want to go where trees are being destroyed.

The serene blue light grew stronger. The trees thinned out and more light spilled between the gaps.

And there was a golden glow as well, though it seemed to rise from the forest floor. And ahead, beyond the trees, the blue grew stronger and wilder.

Trees thinned. The ground became rocky, then salty, then sandy, and then Piper was beyond the trees\=waves battered on a sandy shore. Sunlight sparkled on the waves and the sand and the sea horses, curling and crisping, crashing against the rocks and being dragged back out and then rising, riding again at the shore, crashing and dissipating and reforming.

There were no books: there were no trees for them to be hosted in. But the sunlight sparkled in rainbows on the deep blue of the ocean. The air was salty and tangy and sharp, and carried the scent of water, a coldness she'd not felt in the forest.

She fluttered down to a sharp boulder, jutting steeply out of the golden, stoned, sand.

The sun warmed her wings.

Far, far beyond the curling and crashing waves of blue there was another land, low on the horizon, but visible.

To the north, the beach continued, skirting the edge of the forest until it became lost in the sandy blue of the horizon.

To the south, though that felt like turning back to where she had come from, the beach continued unbroken until it met the sky.

Piper fluttered her wings excitedly. There was a new land, out there, beyond the golden-stroked ocean, beyond the deep waves of blue.

Can I fly that far? Is there somewhere to rest if I am halfway across and my wings grow tired?

She turned to look back at the forest, stretching out behind her.

`I liked the book-forest. I could have spent my life there, happily, if I was someone else.'

She turned back to the ocean and the crashing waves, the rocks that glistened with seaweed and salt, that gleamed, polished bright by the waves.

And the land beyond that, low on the horizon, though she could see peaks and ridges, drops and swells, as if the edge of the land were a mountain.

I can fly up mountains, she thought.

I can fly across an ocean.

I can explore.

Piper took to the wing, beating out across the ocean.
